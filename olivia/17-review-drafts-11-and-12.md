## Article 1: "X% of Executive Tasks Are Now Digital"

### Blocking

**1. The title is still a placeholder.** It literally reads "X%". Whatever number replaces it becomes the article's entire hook, so it has to be one that survives scrutiny. See below, because the two candidates in the draft are both problematic.

**2. The answer box cites a figure that is not in the source.** It says:

> "Microsoft's Work Trend Index shows knowledge workers spend **85% of their time inside digital tools**."

I fetched the cited page and searched it. **"85%" does not appear anywhere on it.**

**3. The body makes a logical error with the same source, and contradicts the answer box.** It says:

> "employees spend 57% of their time on digital communication and 43% on digital creation. **That is 100% of measured working time taking place inside digital tools.**"

What Microsoft actually says, verbatim:

> "**Across the Microsoft 365 apps**, the average employee spends 57% of their time communicating (in meetings, email, and chat) and 43% creating (in documents, spreadsheets, and presentations)."

Those two numbers sum to 100 because they are the only two categories **within Microsoft 365**. It is a split of time already spent in the tools, and says nothing about what share of the workday happens in digital tools. Concluding "100% of working time is digital" from it is circular, and it sits in the same article as an unsupported claim of 85%. A reader who checks either one finds the article does not hold.

**4. Dingel and Neiman measured jobs, not tasks.** Their paper classifies whether an **occupation** can be done entirely at home. The draft states this correctly in the body ("65% of office and administrative support **roles**"), but the title says "X% of executive **tasks**". If 65% becomes the headline number, the title misstates the source. Either change the title to say roles, or find a task-level figure, which is what the McKinsey 2,000-task analysis actually offers.

**5. The Stanford figure is misattributed.** The draft says "a Stanford University study of **16,000 workers**". Nicholas Bloom's experiment ran at Ctrip, a company with roughly 16,000 employees, but the study itself involved **249 volunteers**. "A study of 16,000 workers" is not accurate. The citation also points at his general research page rather than the paper.

### Not blocking, but fix while you are in there

- **Stealth Agents** appears in "How we researched this" but is never cited in the body. It is also a competitor's content marketing. Remove it.
- The cost section repeats the £28,000 to £75,000 and £38,000 to £100,000 figures now live on `/comparison/ea-vs-va-vs-chief-of-staff/`. That is fine as consistency, but check the numbers match exactly rather than approximately.

### Strategic fit

Moderate overlap with `/comparison/outsourced-executive-assistant/`, which already argues the in-house versus outsourced cost case. Manageable, because this article's argument is about **capability and location**, not cost, and the cost section is a supporting passage rather than the thesis. Keep it that way: if the cost section grows, the two pages start competing.

The angle itself is sound and nothing on the site currently makes it.

## Article 2: "Q4 Is the Worst Quarter to Be Your Own Executive Assistant"

### Blocking

**1. A broken internal link.** The draft links to `/guides/what-does-executive-assistant-do/`, which returns **404**. The real page is `/guides/what-does-an-executive-assistant-do/`, with "an".

**2. The APA statistic is not on the cited page.** The draft says:

> "77% of workers reported being stressed by work in the month prior, with 57% experiencing symptoms of burnout (APA, Work in America survey, 2023)"

I fetched that URL. It contains **no "77%", no "57%", and neither the word "stress" nor "burnout"**. Either the figure belongs to a different APA page or the numbers are wrong. It also carries into FAQ 1, so it appears twice.

**3. The Microsoft burnout figure is not on the cited page either.** "53% of managers reported experiencing burnout" is sourced to the same Work Trend Index page used in Article 1. **"53%" does not appear on it.**

**4. The BLS claim does not make sense as written.**

> "a 3.2% productivity spike in Q4, driven by increased output and **longer hours**"

Labour productivity is output **per hour**. Longer hours do not drive productivity up; all else equal they push it down. The sentence describes the opposite of what it claims, and the URL is the generic `bls.gov/lpc/` landing page rather than a specific release, so a reader cannot check it. Cut it or replace it with the actual release and a correct reading.

**5. A cost figure that disagrees with the rest of the site.** FAQ 4 says an in-house hire costs "**€33,000 to €90,000** per year". Article 1 and the live decision guide both say **£28,000 to £75,000** salary, £38,000 to £100,000 fully loaded. Pick one set and use it everywhere.

### Verified and correct

The Lucid Software figure checks out exactly. The source says "a survey of **2,200** knowledge workers worldwide", "**4 in 10** executives are completely confident", "just **17%** of individual contributors share that optimism". The draft reports all three accurately. That is the standard the other statistics need to meet.

### Strategic fit

Good. No cannibalisation: nothing on the site is seasonal, and the Q4 framing does not compete with any existing page. It is genuinely evergreen with an annual date refresh, as Zoran intended. The checklist format is well suited to AI retrieval.

The delegation split section is the strongest writing in either draft.

## The pattern worth naming

Across the two drafts there are **five statistics attributed to pages that do not contain them**, and one that contradicts itself. Every one of them is in a source that is real and reachable, which is what makes it dangerous: the citation looks solid, and only opening the page reveals the figure is not there.

This is the same failure as the Yale statistic in the earlier batch, where a real paper was cited for a number it did not contain. It is not carelessness about sourcing in general, since the sourcing density here is well above the site average and the Lucid citation is exact. It is specifically the step of **confirming the number appears in the page being cited** before writing it down.

Worth making it a fixed rule: open the source, find the figure, copy it, then write the sentence. If the figure cannot be found on the page, the claim does not go in.
