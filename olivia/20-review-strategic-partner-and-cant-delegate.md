# Review: "Strategic Partner" and "5 Things You Can't Delegate"

**Reviewed:** 30 September 2026
**Drafts:** "How to Know If Your Assistant Is a Strategic Partner (Or Just Doing Tasks)" (2,519 words) and "What 5 Things You Can't Delegate to a Virtual Assistant (And What to Do Instead)" (2,052 words)
**Method:** every statistic traced to its source and checked against the source's own wording; every internal link probed for status and robots directive; both drafts checked against live pages for overlap.

---

## Verdict

**The best-sourced pair in this programme. Every checkable statistic passed.** That is the first time both drafts in a batch have cleared without a single fabricated or misplaced figure.

Two things need fixing before publication, and they are different in kind:

- **Article 1 has a cannibalisation problem** that is larger than the brief anticipated, because two existing pages already duplicate each other on the same axis.
- **Article 2 frames a commercial choice as a capability limit**, which undercuts the honesty premise the article is built on. Zoran's warning on the brief was right.

Everything else is small.

## Article 1: the cannibalisation problem

The brief warned that written as another role comparison this would duplicate existing pages. Olivia avoided that trap: the section "This is not about in-house versus virtual" explicitly refuses the comparison framing, which was the right call.

**But the overlap is somewhere else, and it is worse than expected.** Two pages already live on this exact axis:

| Page | Words | Structure |
|---|---|---|
| /ceo-insights/master-the-art-of-working-with-an-assistant/ | 3,141 | "What a BAD Assistant Dynamic Looks Like" / "GOOD" / "GREAT", then "Actionable Tips and Systems" |
| /guides/working-with-executive-assistant/ | 2,459 | "What a Bad Dynamic Looks Like" / "Good" / "Great", then "Systems That Actually Work" |

**Those two already duplicate each other**, almost section for section. That is a pre-existing problem and not Olivia's to solve, but it matters here: this draft would be the third page on the same idea, because task-doer versus strategic partner is the good-versus-great distinction under a different name.

The overlap concentrates in one place. The final section, "How to get more from the assistant you already have" (share context, give permission to nudge, let them sit in on meetings, stop writing briefs, measure outcomes) is roughly 500 words replicating what both existing pages already cover at length.

### The fix

**Cut that section to two or three sentences and link out to both existing pages instead.**

That does three things at once. It removes the duplication. It keeps the article's genuinely distinct contribution, which is the diagnostic ("five signs") plus the in-house objection. And it sends inbound links to `/ceo-insights/master-the-art-of-working-with-an-assistant/`, which currently has **zero** inbound contextual links and is one of the site's orphans.

What survives is the part nothing else on the site does: a self-assessment for a founder who already has an assistant, and a direct attack on the belief that strategic capability requires an in-house hire.

### Separately, for Zoran

The two existing pages need a decision of their own. Near-identical structure, 5,600 words between them, one in `/guides/` and one in `/ceo-insights/`. That is worth resolving regardless of what happens to this draft.

## Article 1: smaller fixes

**1. The McKinsey survey is from November 2011, with 1,374 executives, and the article does not say so.** It is the answer box's load-bearing statistic and it is fifteen years old. This is the same issue as the NIMH figure in the ADHD draft: a real number presented as though current. Add the sample and consider adding the date.

**2. The McKinsey source URL does not resolve.** The cited `.pdf` path fails. Working alternatives are the article page at `/capabilities/people-and-organizational-performance/our-insights/making-time-management-the-organizations-priority` or the `.ashx` version of the same file. A dead citation on a page whose credibility rests on citations is worth thirty seconds to fix.

**3. Slight drift in the paraphrase.** McKinsey says "strong support in scheduling and allocating time". The article says "strong administrative support", which is broader: the source's phrase could cover systems and norms as well as people. Tighten to the source's wording.

**4. The internal link list at the bottom disagrees with the body three ways.**

- The header says "all 6 placed inline" but the list has 7 entries
- Item 7, `/pricing/`, is listed as placed in the "how to get more" section. It is not there
- There are 8 inline `[LINK →]` markers in the body, including `/get-started/`, which is not in the list at all

Reconcile the two before handover, or whoever builds it will place the wrong links.

**5. The in-house PA cost is unsourced.** FAQ 6 says "€40,000 to €75,000 in salary" for a European capital. Every other cost figure across the site traces to Robert Walters (£28,000 to £75,000). Either source this one or align it.

## Article 2: the positioning problem

Zoran's note on the brief was: *"it's thin line what we can and can't do (or won't do)"*. That instinct was correct, and it lands on two of the five items.

### Item 2, cold calling, is a will-not, not a cannot

The body says: *"An executive assistant is not a salesperson."* That is a policy choice presented as a capability boundary.

The article's own FAQ gives it away, because the question is headed **"Why do you not do cold calling?"** rather than "why can you not". The FAQ is honest. The body is not.

This matters more here than it would elsewhere, because the entire article is built on a transparency premise, and the introduction says the point is *"to make the boundaries so clear that everything else becomes obvious delegation territory"*. Saying "cannot" about something that is really "do not" is the one place that premise wobbles, and a reader who notices it will start checking the other four.

**Fix:** reframe item 2 as an explicit choice with the reasoning, exactly as the FAQ already does. Something closer to *"We do not put EAs on cold calling, and here is why."* That is more honest, it is more confident, and it makes the article's honesty claim true rather than approximate.

There is also a commercial risk in leaving it as is: a prospect who wants light outbound support reads "cannot" and goes elsewhere, when the real answer is "not that, but everything around it", which the section already explains well.

### Item 3, job interviews, may be narrower than reality

The article says the EA does not *"sit in the interview chair"*. Worth checking against actual practice. Several DonnaPro EAs run candidate outreach and screening, and Tish reportedly runs candidate outreach for HomeTransformers' sales hiring. If any EA conducts first-round screening calls, this section overstates the boundary and a client will notice.

The other three items (licensed work, specialist technical procurement, physical presence) are clean genuine boundaries. No changes needed.

## Article 2: smaller fixes

**1. "Median" should be "average".** The article says CEOs with high delegation ability *"achieved a three-year median growth rate 112 percentage points higher"*. Gallup says they *"posted an average three-year growth rate of 1,751% -- 112 percentage points greater"*. Average, not median.

**2. "The same research" attributes two figures to the wrong study.** The introduction says Gallup studied 1,400+ entrepreneurs and found one in four has delegation talent, then: *"The same research found that CEOs with high delegation ability achieved a three-year median growth rate 112 percentage points higher... and generated 33% more revenue."*

Those two figures come from the **143 Inc. 500 CEOs** study, not the 1,446-entrepreneur study. Two different samples. The source note at the bottom of the article names both correctly, so the body contradicts the bottom of its own page.

**3. The 75% in the answer box is derived, not quoted.** Gallup says "only one in four... have high Delegator talent". Inverting it to 75% is arithmetically fine but it is not Gallup's number, and the article presents it as "Gallup found that 75%". Safer to use their phrasing: only one in four has strong delegation talent.

**4. "15 to 25 hours per week" is unsourced.** It appears in the conclusion as the amount of operational work a founder could hand over. It is the article's closing claim and it has nothing behind it.

**5. The closing CTA is copied from Article 1 and has no link.** Both drafts end with *"If you are ready for an EA who thinks like a partner, not a task list..."*. In Article 1 that follows naturally from the argument. In Article 2 it follows an article about what an EA cannot do, where it does not fit, and it carries no `[LINK →]` marker at all.

## What is working

Worth saying, because it has taken several rounds to get here.

**Every statistic checked out.** Nine figures across two articles, traced to source, all correct. After batches where real papers were cited for numbers they did not contain, this is the standard.

**Both answer boxes are inside the word limit**, as is every FAQ answer sampled.

**The broken link is gone.** `/guides/what-does-an-executive-assistant-do/` is spelled correctly. That URL was wrong in four consecutive drafts.

**The briefs were followed.** Article 1 explicitly avoids the role-comparison framing that the brief warned against. Article 2 keeps to a short list rather than padding it, which was the whole point of the format.

**Article 2's structure is genuinely good.** "What the EA does instead" under every item turns a list of limitations into a list of capabilities. That is the right instinct and it is well executed.
